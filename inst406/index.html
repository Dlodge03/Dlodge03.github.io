Case study · INST406

Safety you can see and feel.

A phone app and wearable for people who are Deaf or hard of hearing, turning the sounds that matter, like a car approaching or an announcement at the gate, into clear visuals and vibrations. My part was the competitor research that shaped our design direction.

Summary

Today's tools tell you what sound is happening. None of them tell you where the danger is or what to do. Our app leads with street and transit safety: a vehicle-proximity map, labeled vibration alerts and announcement text.

01 — The concept

Sound, turned into signals.

What we're building

A phone app plus a wearable

It listens for important sounds and turns them into visuals and vibration alerts, using map integration and low- and high-frequency audio detection.

Map integrationLow + high frequency detectionVibration alertsVisual labels
Who it's for

People who are Deaf or hard of hearing

Especially when they're out in the world: crossing streets, moving through transit stations and airports.

Problem 01

Crossing the street safely

Including near quiet electric vehicles that are easy to miss.

Problem 02

Missed announcements

Gate changes and updates at airports and on public transportation.

Problem 03

Missed alarms

Alarms and other urgent sounds that need attention right away.

02 — Research

Three competitors, one gap.

I analyzed three tools that help people notice sound at home, at work and in conversation: a hearing-aid app, a built-in phone feature and a haptic wearable. For each, I looked at platform, price, core features, strengths and weaknesses, then pulled out a takeaway for our app.

Competitor 01 · Hearing aid app

Oticon Companion

A free remote control for Oticon hearing aids. It helps people adjust what they hear, but it doesn't turn sounds into visual or haptic alerts.

FreeRequires Oticon hearing aids
PlatformiPhone, iPad, Android, plus Apple Watch control
Core featuresDiscreet volume and program controls, microphone fine-tuning, an equalizer on some models, and "find my hearing aids"
Care + accessoriesRemote visits with an audiologist, usage data for fittings, TV Adapter and ConnectClip

Strengths

  • Polished, discreet design that helps reduce the stigma of hearing aids
  • Connects users to their audiologist through remote care
  • Control from Apple Watch, with step-by-step help and in-app notifications

Weaknesses

  • Only works with Oticon hearing aids, which leaves out Deaf users who don't use them
  • Makes sound easier to hear but never turns it into visual or haptic alerts
  • No hazard detection, vehicle awareness or map-based alerts
  • Features depend on the hearing aid model and firmware
  • The App Store listing doesn't say which accessibility features it supports
Takeaway

Users can switch listening programs for different places. We could offer similar modes, like Street or Transit, that change which alerts and vibrations are active. Remote help from a professional is also worth considering.

Competitor 02 · Built-in feature

Apple Sound Recognition

Apple's built-in accessibility feature listens for set sounds and sends a notification and vibration to the iPhone or the wrist.

FreeRequires Apple hardware
PlatformiPhone and Apple Watch; newer watches can listen even without the iPhone nearby
Core features15 preset sounds, including fire and smoke alarms, sirens, car horns, doorbells, glass breaking, crying babies and pets, plus custom-trained alarms and appliances
Alerts + privacyA notification with a custom tone or haptic pattern for each sound; processing happens on the device

Strengths

  • Already on devices many people own
  • Delivers alerts to the wrist
  • Users choose a haptic pattern for each sound
  • Custom sound training handles unusual alarms

Weaknesses

  • Apple says not to rely on it in high-risk or emergency situations, or for navigation
  • Only 15 preset sound types
  • No direction or distance for a sound
  • Watch-only listening needs newer models
  • Flags that an announcement is happening, but not what it says
Takeaway

Apple falls short exactly where safety matters most: navigation and high-risk moments. Building for those situations gives our design real leverage.

Competitor 03 · Haptic wearable

Neosensory Sound Awareness

A wristband that turns surrounding sounds into vibration patterns on the skin. It's the closest to what we're building.

$999One-time, or 24 interest-free payments of about $42
PlatformA dedicated wristband plus a companion app for iOS and Android
Core featuresA microphone in the band; vibrations change with a sound's pitch and loudness, with adjustable sensitivity and strength
Origin + learningCreated by neuroscientist Dr. David Eagleman with the Deaf community in mind; users learn over time what each vibration means

Strengths

  • A true haptic wearable that works without looking at a screen
  • Splits sound by frequency, so low and high sounds feel different
  • Backed by research and marketed for workplace safety

Weaknesses

  • Very expensive
  • You feel sounds, but they aren't labeled
  • Takes a lot of practice before patterns mean anything
  • No map or location awareness, and no announcement text
  • Another device to buy, charge and wear
Takeaway

Haptics work, but users need meaning, not just a vibration. Our app should pair every vibration pattern with a clear visual label on screen.

04 — Gaps & opportunities

What nobody answers.

Every competitor answers “what sound is happening?” None answers “where is the danger, and what should I do?”

Outdoor safety, not just home alerts

Apple and Neosensory focus on home and work sounds, and Oticon on hearing-aid control. None is built for crossing the street.

Electric vehicles

Apple detects car horns, but no competitor warns you about a silent car moving toward you.

Direction and distance

No competitor shows where a hazard actually is. A map of nearby vehicles fills that gap.

Announcements with meaning

Competitors either capture everything or nothing. Our app can flag that an announcement is happening, then show its text.

Labels plus haptics

Neosensory proves frequency haptics work; Apple proves labeled alerts work. Combining a vibration scale with a clear label is new.

Price

Oticon needs its hearing aids, Apple needs Apple devices and Neosensory costs about $1,000. A cross-platform app with an optional, affordable wearable sits in between.

05 — Design implications

What we’re designing for.

  1. Lead with street safetyThe vehicle-proximity map and crosswalk cues should lead the screens in our mid- and high-fidelity prototypes.
  2. Pair every vibration with a labelFor example, a strong rising vibration plus “Car approaching on the left.”
  3. Let users customize hapticsLike Apple, with different patterns for vehicles, alarms and announcements.
  4. Show announcement textNot just an alert, but the announcement itself in high-contrast text.
  5. Test with Deaf and hard-of-hearing usersRun an accessibility audit at each stage before high fidelity, and test with real users from the community.
